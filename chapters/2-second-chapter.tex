\chapter{Background and State of the Art}
\label{chap:background}

\section*{Introduction}
\phantomsection
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Introduction}

Multi-hop question answering requires a system to retrieve and combine several
pieces of evidence before producing an answer. In Retrieval-Augmented Generation
(RAG), this creates a particular difficulty: the evidence needed for a complex
question may not be easily retrieved from the original query, and different
questions may require very different amounts of retrieval and reasoning.

This chapter introduces the concepts needed for the remainder of the thesis. It
first presents RAG and multi-hop question answering, then reviews retrieval
methods and the notions of evidence coverage and retrieval depth. It subsequently
discusses adaptive computation and question decomposition, before focusing on
decomposition as a means of making evidence easier to retrieve. The chapter ends
by identifying the two research gaps addressed in the following chapters.

\section[Multi-Hop Question Answering and Retrieval-Augmented Generation]
{Multi-Hop Question Answering and\\Retrieval-Augmented Generation}
\label{sec:rag-multihop}

\subsection{Retrieval-Augmented Generation}
\label{subsec:rag}

Large Language Models (LLMs) acquire substantial knowledge during pre-training,
but their parametric knowledge is neither a complete nor a continuously updated
record of the world. It may omit specialised facts, become outdated, or be
insufficient when a question requires information from a particular source.
RAG complements the model with an external corpus that can be searched at
inference time~\cite{lewis2020rag}.

Let the corpus be
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{C}=\{d_1,d_2,\ldots,d_N\},
  \label{eq:corpus}
\end{equation}
where each \(d_i\) is a document or passage. For a query \(q\), a retriever
assigns a relevance score \(s(q,d)\) and returns
\begin{equation}
  D_k(q)=\operatorname*{TopK}_{d\in\mathcal{C}} s(q,d).
  \label{eq:top-k}
\end{equation}
The generator then predicts an answer \(y\) conditional on the question and the
retrieved passages,
\begin{equation}
  p\bigl(y\mid q,D_k(q)\bigr).
  \label{eq:rag-generation}
\end{equation}
This division of labour is central: the retriever determines which external
information becomes visible, while the generator interprets and combines that
information. Figure~\ref{fig:rag-pipeline} depicts the separation between the
two components. A stronger generator can reason more effectively over retrieved
context, but it cannot directly exploit a relevant passage that the retriever
never exposes.

The retrieval cutoff \(k\) controls both opportunity and cost. A larger context
may contain more useful evidence, but also introduces distractors and increases
the amount of text processed by the generator. This tension is mild when one
passage directly answers the question and more consequential when the answer
depends on several passages with different lexical and semantic relations to the
original query.

\subsection{Multi-Hop Question Answering}
\label{subsec:multihop-qa}

A single-hop question can normally be resolved through one principal factual
relation or evidence source. A multi-hop question instead requires several
dependent facts or operations,
\begin{equation}
  r_1 \longrightarrow r_2 \longrightarrow \cdots \longrightarrow r_h,
  \label{eq:reasoning-chain}
\end{equation}
where an intermediate result may determine what must be resolved next. The
entity or value transferred between two steps is called a \emph{bridge entity}.
For example, the question \emph{Which country is the birthplace of the author of
Book~X?} first requires identifying the author, then retrieving that person's
birthplace. Figure~\ref{fig:multihop-chain} makes this dependency explicit.

Reasoning hop count and retrieval depth describe different quantities and must
not be conflated:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\text{reasoning hop count}\neq\text{retrieval depth}.}
  \label{eq:hops-not-depth}
\end{equation}
A four-hop benchmark question encodes four dependent reasoning operations.
Retrieval depth \(k=50\), by contrast, means that up to fifty ranked passages are
considered for a query. Later chapters seek to reduce the latter; they do not
alter the benchmark's compositional hop count.

\subsection{Multi-Hop Question Answering Benchmarks}
\label{subsec:multihop-benchmarks}

Multi-hop benchmarks operationalise compositional reasoning in different ways.
Three datasets are particularly relevant to this thesis. \textbf{HotpotQA} is a
Wikipedia-based benchmark designed for diverse, explainable multi-hop question
answering. In addition to answers, it annotates supporting facts, enabling joint
evaluation of answer prediction and evidence identification~\cite{yang2018hotpotqa}.

\textbf{2WikiMultiHopQA} constructs multi-hop questions from Wikipedia and
Wikidata information and provides explicit evidence and reasoning structures.
Its question types include comparison and compositional chains, allowing models
to be assessed at intermediate as well as final steps~\cite{ho2020constructing}.

\textbf{MuSiQue} is constructed bottom-up by composing connected single-hop
questions into questions requiring two to four hops. Its construction preserves
dependencies among intermediate questions and supporting passages, while
filtering examples that can be answered through shortcuts~\cite{trivedi2022musique}.
This structured relation between intermediate questions and evidence is also
useful when studying how generated decompositions divide responsibility for
retrieving the evidence.

The three benchmarks therefore provide distinct operational views of
compositional difficulty. They form the empirical basis of the layer-wise
probing study introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:survey}~\cite{abdelhedi2026probing},
without assuming that a nominal hop label is a complete measure of all forms of
difficulty.

\subsection{Evidence and Supporting Passages}
\label{subsec:evidence}

Let the gold evidence for a question be
\begin{equation}
  E(q)=\{e_1,e_2,\ldots,e_m\},
  \label{eq:gold-evidence}
\end{equation}
and let the retriever produce the ordered ranking
\begin{equation}
  R(q)=(d_1,d_2,\ldots,d_N).
  \label{eq:retrieval-ranking}
\end{equation}
For \(e\in E(q)\), \(\operatorname{rank}(e\mid q)\) denotes its one-based
position in this ranking. Evidence retrieval is successful only to the extent
that the required supporting passages appear within the part of the ranking made
available downstream.

Answer correctness and evidence retrieval success are related but not
equivalent. An LLM can occasionally answer correctly from parametric memory even
when the annotated supporting passages are absent. Conversely, retrieving the
gold passages does not guarantee that the generator will combine them correctly.
For this reason, the later evaluation isolates whether required evidence is
available instead of inferring retrieval quality solely from the final answer.

\section{Retrieval for Multi-Hop Reasoning}
\label{sec:retrieval}

Retrieval methods differ chiefly in how they represent queries and passages and
how they compute the score \(s(q,d)\). Sparse methods emphasise lexical overlap,
dense methods learn semantic vector representations, and late-interaction
methods retain token-level matching. These choices determine the ranking on
which evidence coverage and retrieval depth are measured.

\subsection{Sparse Retrieval}
\label{subsec:sparse-retrieval}

Sparse retrieval represents text using high-dimensional term vectors. In a
Term Frequency--Inverse Document Frequency (TF--IDF) representation, the weight
of term \(t\) in document \(d\) is
\begin{equation}
  w(t,d)=\operatorname{tf}(t,d)\operatorname{idf}(t),
  \qquad
  \operatorname{idf}(t)=\log\frac{N}{\operatorname{df}(t)},
  \label{eq:tfidf}
\end{equation}
where \(\operatorname{tf}(t,d)\) is the term frequency and
\(\operatorname{df}(t)\) counts documents containing \(t\). Query and document
vectors can then be compared with a cosine-style similarity. TF--IDF assigns
greater influence to terms that are frequent in a document but rare throughout
the corpus~\cite{salton1988term}.

BM25 refines lexical scoring by saturating term-frequency gains and normalising
for document length~\cite{robertson2009bm25}:
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{BM25}(q,d)=
  \sum_{t\in q}\operatorname{IDF}(t)
  \frac{f(t,d)(k_1+1)}
  {f(t,d)+k_1\left(1-b+b\frac{|d|}{\operatorname{avgdl}}\right)}.
  \label{eq:bm25}
\end{equation}
Here \(f(t,d)\) is the term frequency, \(k_1\) controls saturation, \(b\)
controls document-length normalisation, and \(\operatorname{avgdl}\) is average
document length. Sparse indices are efficient and interpretable, and exact
entity names often provide strong signals. Their principal limitation is lexical
mismatch: a relevant passage can express the needed relation without sharing
the query's wording, especially when the original question hides an intermediate
entity.

\subsection{Dense Retrieval}
\label{subsec:dense-retrieval}

Dense retrieval learns encoders that map text into a continuous vector space.
Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR), for example, uses separate question and passage
encoders~\cite{karpukhin2020dpr}:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{q}=f_q(q),
  \qquad
  \mathbf{d}=f_d(d),
  \qquad
  s(q,d)=\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{d}.
  \label{eq:dpr}
\end{equation}
Passage vectors can be precomputed and searched with approximate nearest-neighbour
methods. Because the representation is learned, semantically related text can
match despite limited word overlap. This is valuable when a question and its
evidence use different expressions.

The compact representation also creates a bottleneck. A single vector must
compress all potentially relevant entities and relations in a passage. For a
complex query that simultaneously implies several information needs, the dot
product provides only a coarse aggregate match and may obscure which part of the
query aligns with which part of a passage.

\subsection{Late-Interaction Retrieval and Reranking}
\label{subsec:late-interaction}

Contextualized Late Interaction over BERT (ColBERT) occupies a middle ground
between lexical matching and single-vector dense retrieval
~\cite{khattab2020colbert}. It independently encodes contextual
query tokens \(\mathbf{q}_i\) and document tokens \(\mathbf{d}_j\), then applies
a maximum-similarity operation for every query token:
\begin{equation}
  s_{\mathrm{ColBERT}}(q,d)
  =\sum_i\max_j\mathbf{q}_i^{\top}\mathbf{d}_j.
  \label{eq:colbert}
\end{equation}
This \emph{MaxSim} interaction preserves fine-grained matches while allowing
document representations to be precomputed. It is relevant to the later
arm--evidence attribution problem because deciding whether a subquestion is
responsible for a passage is partly a fine-grained query--passage matching task.

Retrieval systems also commonly separate inexpensive candidate generation from
more expensive scoring. A fast retriever first produces a manageable candidate
set; a cross-encoder or another stronger reranker jointly examines each
query--passage pair and reorders that set. Reranking can improve precision near
the top but cannot recover a gold passage omitted from the candidate set. The
initial retrieval stage therefore continues to constrain attainable evidence
coverage.

\subsection{Iterative and Multi-Hop Retrieval}
\label{subsec:iterative-retrieval}

Single-pass retrieval uses the same original question to search for every
supporting passage. Iterative retrieval instead allows evidence acquired at one
step to change the next query. Multi-Hop Dense Retrieval (MDR) recursively
retrieves passages with a dense representation conditioned on the question and
earlier evidence, enabling later searches to incorporate newly identified bridge
information~\cite{xiong2021mdr}.

Baleen addresses the rapid growth of the multi-hop search space through
\emph{condensed retrieval}: after each iteration, salient information from the
retrieved material is condensed into a compact context for subsequent search.
It combines this mechanism with focused late interaction and a learned ordering
of retrieval steps~\cite{khattab2021baleen}.

Interleaving Retrieval with Chain-of-Thought Reasoning (IRCoT) alternates the two
operations. An intermediate
reasoning step specifies what information is needed next; retrieved evidence then
updates the context used to produce the following step~\cite{trivedi2023interleaving}.
These approaches differ architecturally, but share a crucial observation:
retrieval can benefit from intermediate state rather than repeatedly treating
the initial question as a fixed search query.

\begin{center}
  \fbox{\parbox{0.86\textwidth}{\centering
    The original full question is not necessarily the best retrieval query for
    every evidence passage.}}
\end{center}
This observation motivates the later shift from implicit intermediate state to
explicit question decomposition.

\subsection{Evidence Coverage and Retrieval Depth}
\label{subsec:coverage-depth}

For the top-\(k\) passages \(D_k(q)\), evidence coverage is the fraction of gold
passages retrieved:
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Coverage}@k(q)
  =\frac{|E(q)\cap D_k(q)|}{|E(q)|}.
  \label{eq:coverage-at-k}
\end{equation}
Complete recovery at a cutoff can be recorded as
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Complete}(q,k)
  =\mathbb{1}\!\left[E(q)\subseteq D_k(q)\right].
  \label{eq:complete-at-k}
\end{equation}
When all required evidence is present in the ranking, the minimum depth at which
it has been recovered is determined by the lowest-ranked required passage:
\begin{equation}
  K^{\star}(q)=\max_{e\in E(q)}\operatorname{rank}(e\mid q).
  \label{eq:min-complete-depth}
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:ranked-evidence} illustrates these quantities. At \(k=5\), two
of three gold passages have been recovered, so coverage is \(2/3\). Complete
coverage is first reached at \(K^{\star}=7\).

If at least one required passage does not occur within the evaluated search
horizon, the question is \emph{uncovered}; assigning it an artificial finite
depth would falsely make failure appear efficient. \(K^{\star}\) is thus defined
for questions whose evidence is recovered, while coverage separately accounts
for failures. It is a rank-based proxy for retrieval effort, not a reasoning hop
count and not a direct measurement of wall-clock computation. Actual latency
also depends on indexing, model architecture, batching, and hardware.

Coverage and depth must therefore be evaluated jointly. A useful retrieval
strategy should expose as much of the required evidence as possible and do so
near the top of its rankings:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\text{high evidence coverage}\quad+\quad\text{low retrieval depth}.}
  \label{eq:coverage-depth-goal}
\end{equation}
Here \emph{evidence coverage} means the recovery of annotated supporting
passages. It is distinct from \emph{conformal} or \emph{statistical coverage},
which concerns the long-run validity of prediction sets or risk guarantees in
uncertainty-quantification methods.

Because the depth required for complete evidence recovery varies substantially
from one question to another, applying a uniformly expensive retrieval policy is
neither necessary nor efficient. This observation leads to systems that estimate
what an input needs and adapt their computation accordingly.

\section{Difficulty-Aware and Adaptive Computation}
\label{sec:adaptive-computation}

Fixed pipelines allocate the same retrieval and reasoning procedure to every
input, although a direct factual question and a four-step compositional question
have different needs. Adaptive systems instead obtain a signal about the input
or current state and translate it into an action:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\text{signal}\longrightarrow\text{adaptive computational action}.}
  \label{eq:signal-action}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Query Complexity and Routing}
\label{subsec:complexity-routing}

Adaptive-RAG demonstrates complexity-aware routing for question answering. A
classifier estimates whether a question is best handled without retrieval, with
single-step retrieval, or with an iterative strategy, thereby avoiding the cost
of a uniformly complex pipeline~\cite{jeong2024adaptiverag}. In generic form,
\begin{equation}
  q\longrightarrow\widehat{d}(q)\longrightarrow\pi_{\widehat{d}},
  \label{eq:difficulty-routing}
\end{equation}
where \(\widehat{d}(q)\) is an estimated difficulty or complexity level and
\(\pi_{\widehat{d}}\) is the selected computational policy. The important point
is not a particular number of routing classes, but that a pre-answer estimate
can govern retrieval, reasoning, or decomposition before their full costs are
paid.

Existing routing approaches establish that question complexity is actionable.
They also motivate a representational question: instead of estimating difficulty
only from surface characteristics with an external classifier, could the target
LLM itself already encode this information internally? This is the question
taken up in Chapter~\ref{chap:survey}.

\subsection{Adaptive Retrieval During Generation}
\label{subsec:adaptive-generation}

Adaptation can also occur after generation begins. Forward-Looking Active
REtrieval augmented generation (FLARE) anticipates the next sentence, examines
confidence in the predicted content, and triggers retrieval
when low-confidence tokens indicate that more information is needed. The
retrieved documents are then used to regenerate the sentence~\cite{jiang2023flare}.
Self-RAG trains a model to produce reflection signals that control retrieval on
demand and support critique of both evidence and generated text
~\cite{asai2023selfrag}.

These systems illustrate a broader design principle identified in the Trust
Layer survey conducted during the internship: signals become operationally
useful when connected to actions such as retrieval, re-retrieval, routing,
stopping, filtering, or abstention~\cite{heidari2026trustlayer}. The present
thesis adopts this signal-to-action perspective, but does not require a full
taxonomy of uncertainty quantification. Its focus is the allocation of retrieval
and decomposition effort for multi-hop questions.

\subsection{Difficulty and Uncertainty}
\label{subsec:difficulty-uncertainty}

Difficulty and uncertainty can influence the same adaptive action, but they are
not interchangeable:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\text{difficulty}\neq\text{uncertainty}.}
  \label{eq:difficulty-not-uncertainty}
\end{equation}
\emph{Difficulty} describes the amount or complexity of reasoning structurally
required by a question. \emph{Uncertainty} describes the reliability of the
system's current prediction, retrieval state, or answer. A difficult question
may be answered confidently when all necessary evidence and reasoning are
available; an easy question may remain uncertain because retrieval failed. The
other combinations---difficult and uncertain, or easy and confident---are also
possible. Chapter~\ref{chap:survey} therefore studies compositional difficulty
from question representations before answer generation, rather than estimating
uncertainty in an answer that has already been produced.

Estimating difficulty is only useful if the estimate changes what the system does.
For a question predicted to require compositional reasoning, one possible action
is to expose its latent information needs explicitly through decomposition.

\section{Question Decomposition for Multi-Hop Reasoning}
\label{sec:decomposition}

Question decomposition makes the information needs of a complex question
explicit. Let
\begin{equation}
  D(q)=\{a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_m\},
  \label{eq:decomposition}
\end{equation}
where each \(a_i\) is a simpler subquestion. In the remainder of this thesis,
generated subquestions are also called \emph{decomposition arms}. The notation
does not imply that arms are mutually independent: some can require answers
obtained by earlier arms.

\subsection{Dependency-Aware Decomposition}
\label{subsec:dependency-aware}

In a chained decomposition, execution alternates between asking a subquestion
and obtaining an intermediate answer:
\begin{equation}
  a_1\longrightarrow z_1\longrightarrow a_2(z_1)
  \longrightarrow z_2\longrightarrow a_3(z_2),
  \label{eq:chained-decomposition}
\end{equation}
where \(z_i\) is an intermediate answer or bridge entity. Before \(z_i\) is
known, a downstream arm can represent the unresolved dependency abstractly;
execution resolves dependencies in order and supplies the available information.
The final downstream arm obtains what is needed to answer the original question.

More generally, dependencies form a directed acyclic graph (DAG),
\begin{equation}
  G=(V,E),\qquad V=\{a_1,\ldots,a_m\},
  \label{eq:decomposition-dag}
\end{equation}
whose directed edges identify which arms supply information to others. A chain
is a special case of a DAG; branching allows independent information needs to
be resolved in parallel and later combined. Figure~\ref{fig:decomposition-dag}
shows a generic branching example without prescribing a particular generation
format or execution mechanism.

\subsection{The Compositionality Gap}
\label{subsec:compositionality-gap}

Explicit decomposition is motivated partly by the \emph{compositionality gap}:
a model can answer the component questions needed for a problem yet fail when
they are presented as one composed question. Press et al.\ show that this gap is
not necessarily eliminated by model scaling and introduce Self-Ask, in which the
model generates and answers explicit follow-up questions before producing its
final response~\cite{press2023compositionality}. The result is important here not
as a claim that decomposition always succeeds, but as evidence that exposing
intermediate information needs can change a model's ability to use knowledge it
already possesses or can retrieve.

\subsection{Earlier Question-Decomposition Approaches}
\label{subsec:earlier-decomposition}

Question decomposition predates modern instruction-tuned LLMs. Min et al.\
decompose multi-hop reading-comprehension questions into simpler single-hop
questions, answer them with existing readers, and globally rescore candidate
decompositions and answers. Their method reduces the amount of dedicated
supervision by treating subquestion generation as span prediction
~\cite{min2019multihop}.

Perez et al.\ propose an unsupervised one-to-many sequence-transduction approach
that learns to produce single-hop subquestions without explicit decomposition
labels. Answers from an off-the-shelf question-answering model are subsequently recomposed into
the final answer~\cite{perez2020unsupervised}. Together, these methods establish
decomposition as an intermediate representation that can connect a complex
question to reusable single-hop components.

\subsection{LLM-Based Decomposition}
\label{subsec:llm-decomposition}

The prompting capabilities of LLMs enable decomposition without a fixed
task-specific parser. Successive Prompting repeatedly generates a simpler
question, answers it, and uses the result to continue until the complex problem
is resolved~\cite{dua2022successive}. Least-to-Most Prompting first decomposes a
problem and then solves the resulting subproblems sequentially, allowing earlier
solutions to support later ones~\cite{zhou2023leasttomost}. Decomposed Prompting
treats the solution as a modular program in which a controller delegates
subproblems to specialised prompts or modules~\cite{khot2023decomposed}.

Their common pattern is
\begin{center}
  \fbox{\parbox{0.84\textwidth}{\centering
    complex question \(\longrightarrow\) explicit simpler subproblems
    \(\longrightarrow\) dependency-aware solution
    \(\longrightarrow\) final aggregation.}}
\end{center}
Because an LLM's decomposition is generated rather than fixed, it is itself a
configurable reasoning component. Different instructions or examples can expose
different subproblems, and decomposition quality consequently affects all later
retrieval and reasoning stages.

\subsection{From Answer-Oriented to Retrieval-Oriented Decomposition}
\label{subsec:retrieval-oriented-decomposition}

Most decomposition methods are primarily evaluated through downstream task or
answer performance. The perspective adopted in this thesis adds a narrower
question: \emph{does the decomposition make the required evidence easier to
retrieve?} Suppose a supporting passage \(e\) appears deep in the full-question
ranking but near the top for a targeted arm:
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{rank}(e\mid q)=80,
  \qquad
  \operatorname{rank}(e\mid a_i)=4.
  \label{eq:rank-improvement-example}
\end{equation}
The underlying reasoning dependency has not disappeared. Rather, the arm states
a narrower information need and can remove lexical or semantic interference from
unrelated parts of the full question.

This perspective does not imply that previous work ignores retrieval. Self-Ask
can call a search engine for follow-up questions, and IRCoT explicitly couples
intermediate reasoning with search~\cite{press2023compositionality,trivedi2023interleaving}.
The contribution developed later is more specific: it makes evidence coverage
and maximum retrieval depth explicit criteria for evaluating and optimising a
generated decomposition.

\section{Adaptive Decomposition and Efficient Evidence Retrieval}
\label{sec:adaptive-decomposition}

If decomposition is to improve retrieval, it must expose each evidence need
without discarding difficult passages. This requires more than retrieving
separately for every arm: the evaluation must know which arm is expected to
retrieve which piece of evidence and must aggregate those arm-level results into
a valid question-level measure.

\subsection{The Evidence-Responsibility Problem}
\label{subsec:evidence-responsibility}

Consider a decomposition \(D(q)=\{a_1,\ldots,a_m\}\). Its gold evidence is
\(E(q)=\{e_1,\ldots,e_n\}\). When retrieving with the full question, all
evidence is associated with a single query. Under decomposition, evaluating every arm
against every passage would penalise a focused arm for evidence unrelated to its
subproblem. Let the responsibility relation be
\begin{equation}
  z_{ij}=
  \begin{cases}
    1, & \text{if arm \(a_i\) is responsible for evidence \(e_j\),}\\
    0, & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:responsibility}
\end{equation}
The evidence assigned to arm \(a_i\) is then
\begin{equation}
  E_i=\{e_j:z_{ij}=1\}.
  \label{eq:arm-evidence}
\end{equation}
For an arm whose assigned evidence is retrieved, its required depth is
\begin{equation}
  K_i=\max_{e\in E_i}\operatorname{rank}(e\mid a_i),
  \label{eq:arm-depth}
\end{equation}
and the decomposition's maximum depth, taken over arms with non-empty assigned
evidence, is
\begin{equation}
  K_{\mathrm{decomp}}=\max_i K_i.
  \label{eq:decomposition-depth}
\end{equation}
As with \(K^{\star}\), uncovered assignments must be recorded as coverage
failures rather than silently given a favourable finite depth.

The central difficulty is determining \(z_{ij}\) for arbitrary generated
decompositions, whose wording and number of arms are not fixed in advance.
MuSiQue is useful for developing this evaluation because its constructed
question graph relates intermediate questions to supporting evidence
~\cite{trivedi2022musique}. Chapter~\ref{chap:empirical-studies} develops the
evidence-aware evaluator and arm--evidence attribution mechanism; the present
chapter deliberately leaves its implementation unspecified.

\subsection{Coverage--Depth Trade-Off}
\label{subsec:coverage-depth-tradeoff}

A small depth is meaningful only when the associated evidence has actually been
recovered. Consider a method A with high coverage and maximum depth 10, and a
method B with lower coverage and maximum depth 3. Method B is not automatically
better: its shallow depth may be an artefact of missing the passages that are
hardest to retrieve. The objectives are therefore simultaneous,
\begin{equation}
  \max\ \operatorname{Coverage},
  \qquad
  \min\ K_{\max}.
  \label{eq:multiobjective}
\end{equation}
At equal coverage, lower depth is preferable; at equal depth, higher coverage is
preferable.

problem. The points are illustrative methods, not experimental measurements.

trade-off rather than collapsing failures and efficiency into one number.

\subsection{Evaluating the Quality of a Decomposition}
\label{subsec:decomposition-quality}

A retrieval-oriented evaluator for an arbitrary decomposition must answer five
questions. First, does the set of arms preserve the evidence required by the
original question? Second, is each passage assigned to an appropriate arm?
Third, does that arm retrieve its evidence at a shallower rank? Fourth, can the
arm-level results be aggregated without hiding uncovered evidence? Finally, does
the resulting question-level metric provide a useful signal for improving the
decomposer?

Existing work demonstrates that decomposition can improve reasoning and that
intermediate queries can guide retrieval. The perspective adopted in this thesis
is to make evidence coverage and maximum retrieval depth explicit evaluation and
optimisation criteria for the decomposition. Chapter~\ref{chap:empirical-studies}
develops the evaluator and arm--evidence attribution, while
Chapter~\ref{chap:guarantees} uses this evaluation as feedback for prompt
optimisation.

\section{Research Gap and Thesis Positioning}
\label{sec:research-gap}

The preceding literature connects complex questions to adaptive computation and
decomposition, but leaves two questions that structure the experimental chapters
of this thesis.

\subsection{Anticipating Compositional Difficulty}
\label{subsec:gap-difficulty}

Adaptive systems use query complexity or runtime confidence to select different
policies. Many signals come from external classifiers, surface properties, or
states observed only after generation has begun. This thesis instead asks
whether the internal representation formed by the target LLM already contains a
usable pre-answer signal about the question's compositional demands.

\begin{center}
  \fbox{\parbox{0.88\textwidth}{\centering
    \textbf{Does the LLM already encode how much compositional reasoning a
    question requires?}}}
\end{center}

Chapter~\ref{chap:survey} addresses this question through layer-wise probing of
question representations. It separates compositional difficulty from expected
answer correctness and evaluates the former without requiring an answer to be
generated first.

\subsection{Optimizing Decomposition for Retrieval Efficiency}
\label{subsec:gap-efficient-decomposition}

Question decomposition exposes intermediate information needs, and iterative
retrieval demonstrates that such intermediate state can improve search. The
remaining issue is whether an arbitrary generated decomposition can be judged
and improved specifically as a set of retrieval queries.

\begin{center}
  \fbox{\parbox{0.88\textwidth}{\centering
    \textbf{Can a decomposition preserve evidence coverage while reducing the
    maximum depth required to retrieve that evidence?}}}
\end{center}

Answering this requires three capabilities: determining which evidence each arm
is responsible for, evaluating decompositions jointly through coverage and
depth, and adapting the decomposition-generation strategy according to those
metrics. Chapter~\ref{chap:empirical-studies} develops the evidence-aware
evaluation and attribution required by the first two. Chapter~\ref{chap:guarantees}
then uses the resulting signal to optimise the decomposition prompt.

\section*{Conclusion}
\phantomsection
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Conclusion}

This chapter presented the background necessary for the experimental work in
the remainder of the thesis. Multi-hop question answering requires both
reasoning across several pieces of evidence and retrieval mechanisms capable of
exposing that evidence efficiently. Existing work approaches these demands
through stronger retrievers, iterative search, adaptive computation, and
question decomposition.

Two questions remain central. The first is whether multi-hop difficulty can be
identified directly from the model before answering, which is investigated in
Chapter~\ref{chap:survey}. The second is whether decomposition can be evaluated
and optimised to preserve evidence coverage while reducing retrieval depth,
which motivates Chapters~\ref{chap:empirical-studies} and~\ref{chap:guarantees}.
