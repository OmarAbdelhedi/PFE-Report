\section*{Introduction}
\phantomsection
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Introduction}

The purpose of this chapter is to situate the project within its general research and institutional context, 
and to present the main objectives, research directions, and methodology that guided the work carried out during the internship.

The work reported in this thesis was conducted as a research internship at the AI for Decision Making Lab of Concordia University, 
under the supervision of Dr. Yassine Yaakoubi, and constitutes the final-year project of the engineering curriculum at 
École Polytechnique de Tunisie (EPT).


This chapter first introduces the host institution and the research laboratory in which the internship was carried out, 
together with their main areas of research. It then presents the broader scientific context of the internship, 
centered on large language models, retrieval-augmented generation, and multi-hop question answering. 
The progression of the research work is subsequently outlined, from the initial literature exploration and systematic study of 
trust signals in RAG systems, to the investigation of compositional question difficulty in language model representations,
and finally to the development and optimisation of a retrieval-oriented framework for multi-hop question decomposition. 
The chapter concludes by presenting the main objectives, contributions, and research methodology adopted throughout the internship.

\subsection{Concordia University}
\label{subsec:ch1-concordia}

Concordia University is a public comprehensive research university located in Montreal, Quebec. 
It was officially founded in 1974 following the merger of Loyola College, established in 1896, 
and Sir George Williams University, whose origins can be traced to the educational activities of the 
Montreal YMCA~\citeN{net-merger,net-sgw}. The name of the university derives from the motto of the City 
of Montreal, \emph{Concordia salus}, meaning ``well-being through harmony.''

Concordia operates two main campuses: the Sir George Williams Campus in downtown Montreal and the 
Loyola Campus in the west of the city. In the 2024--2025 academic year, 43,922 students were enrolled in 
credit courses across the university \citeN{net-fastfacts}. Its academic activities cover a broad range of 
disciplines through the Faculty of Arts and Science, the Gina Cody School of Engineering and Computer Science, 
the John Molson School of Business, and the Faculty of Fine Arts.

\subsection{Research group, supervision, and research axes}
\label{subsec:ch1-research-group}

The work presented in this report was conducted within the AI for Decision Making Lab (AI4DM), 
led by Dr.\ Yassine Yaakoubi, Assistant Professor in the Department of Mechanical, Industrial and 
Aerospace Engineering at Concordia University.

The research activities of the group lie at the intersection of optimisation, machine learning, 
and simulation for large-scale real-world decision making. The group combines mathematical optimisation 
methods, including mixed-integer and stochastic programming and decomposition techniques, with machine-learning 
approaches such as deep learning, reinforcement learning, and graph-based models. The general objective is to 
design decision-support systems that remain efficient, robust, and scalable in complex and uncertain environments, 
with applications including logistics, transportation, supply chains, and sustainability.

A major part of the group's work is organised around machine-learning-augmented optimisation, where learned models 
are integrated into classical optimisation procedures, and end-to-end optimisation learning, where learning 
mechanisms are used more directly to guide decision making.

A further strand of the group's research activity extends these concerns with robustness, uncertainty, and 
adaptive decision making to systems built on large language models. This includes retrieval-augmented generation, 
where language models are grounded in evidence retrieved from external knowledge sources, as well as uncertainty 
quantification, reliability assessment, and adaptive behaviour in such systems.

The work carried out during this internship belongs primarily to this latter research direction. It focuses on 
multi-hop question answering in retrieval-augmented systems and investigates, from complementary perspectives, 
how the difficulty of such questions can be understood and how their processing can be made more effective. The 
research therefore evolved from the study of reliability and actionable signals in RAG systems, to the analysis of 
compositional question difficulty in the internal representations of large language models, and finally to the development and optimisation of a retrieval-oriented approach to multi-hop question decomposition.

This research environment provided the basis for the successive studies conducted during the internship and for the 
formulation of the main research problem addressed in this thesis.

\section{Problem statement}
\label{sec:ch1-problem-statement}

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) improves the ability of Large Language
Models (LLMs) to answer knowledge-intensive questions by providing information
retrieved from an external corpus. However, this paradigm becomes more
challenging when a question requires several dependent pieces of evidence.
In multi-hop question answering, the information needed to resolve one part of
a question may depend on the result of an earlier reasoning step, and some
supporting passages may have only a weak lexical or semantic relation to the
original question.

A second difficulty is that multi-hop questions do not all require the same
amount of reasoning or retrieval. Applying the same computational strategy to
every question may therefore be inefficient: simple questions may receive
unnecessary processing, while more compositional questions may require
additional reasoning or decomposition before their evidence can be retrieved
effectively. This raises the question of whether the difficulty of a question
can be anticipated before answer generation and used as a signal for adaptive
processing.

Question decomposition provides one possible response to difficult
compositional questions. By converting a complex question into simpler
subquestions, it makes intermediate information needs explicit and can provide
more focused retrieval queries. Nevertheless, generating a decomposition does
not guarantee that the resulting subquestions retrieve all of the evidence
required by the original question. Moreover, final-answer accuracy alone does
not reveal whether a failure originated from missing evidence, an inappropriate
decomposition, an incorrect intermediate answer, or the final reader.

The project therefore addresses two complementary problems. The first concerns
the input question itself: whether compositional difficulty is already reflected
in the internal representations of an LLM before it generates an answer. The
second concerns the processing of a question once decomposition is used: how to
evaluate whether its subquestions expose the required evidence, and how to
improve the decomposition and allocation of retrieval resources accordingly.

These two directions define the central problem of this thesis: making
multi-hop question answering more adaptive and evidence-aware by identifying
signals of compositional demand and by evaluating and improving the retrieval
behaviour induced by question decomposition.



\section{Objectives and expected deliverables}
\label{sec:ch1-objectives}


The general objective of the internship was to investigate how multi-hop
question answering systems can better adapt their retrieval and reasoning
behaviour to the compositional requirements of a question. The work was divided
into three main objectives.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Investigate compositional difficulty in LLM
    representations.}
    Study whether the internal representations produced while an LLM reads a
    question contain a linearly decodable signal associated with
    benchmark-defined compositional difficulty, and evaluate the robustness of
    this signal across models, benchmarks, and validation controls.

    \item \textbf{Develop an evidence-aware evaluation framework for question
    decomposition.}
    Design measurements that distinguish final-answer correctness from
    retrieval success, quantify how deeply supporting evidence must be
    retrieved, and estimate which subquestions are responsible for retrieving
    which supporting passages.

    \item \textbf{Study mechanisms for improving decomposition and retrieval
    allocation.}
    Use the resulting retrieval-oriented measurements to investigate automated
    decomposition-instruction search and the allocation of a limited passage
    budget across the subquestions of a generated decomposition.
\end{enumerate}

The expected deliverables therefore included an experimental pipeline for
probing question representations, an evidence-aware framework for analysing
multi-hop decompositions, an implementation of evidence-ownership estimation,
experiments on automated prompt search and passage allocation, and a documented
analysis of their limitations and possible extensions.

The research conducted during the internship also led to scientific
dissemination through conference work related to the questions investigated in
this thesis. In particular, the compositional-difficulty probing study presented
in Chapter~3 was accepted for publication, and the corresponding experimental
pipeline and analysis form one of the principal research outputs of the
internship.

\section{Working methodology}
\label{sec:ch1-methodology}

The internship followed an iterative research methodology in which literature
analysis, experimental implementation, evaluation, and refinement informed one
another. Rather than starting from a fixed end-to-end architecture, the work
progressively identified and studied several components required for adaptive
multi-hop question answering.

The first stage consisted of a review of retrieval-augmented generation,
reliability and uncertainty in RAG systems, adaptive computation, multi-hop
reasoning, and question decomposition. This phase helped identify the distinction
between signals that describe the reliability of an answer and signals that
describe the structural difficulty of the input question. It motivated the
first experimental direction of the internship: probing LLM representations for
compositional difficulty.

The second stage focused on representation analysis. Hidden states were
extracted from frozen language models for questions of different
benchmark-defined difficulty levels. Simple linear probes and several validation
controls were then used to determine whether difficulty-related information was
decodable before answer generation. The resulting study is presented in
Chapter~3.

The third stage moved from anticipating difficulty to the execution of
decomposition. A sequential multi-hop retrieval pipeline was implemented and
analysed on benchmark questions with annotated supporting evidence. This phase
led to the development of retrieval-depth and evidence-coverage measurements,
together with an evidence-ownership estimator for analysing which subquestions
are associated with which supporting passages. These methods and their
validation form Chapter~4.

The final stage investigated how the measurements developed in the preceding
stage could be made actionable. Automated search over decomposition
instructions was evaluated using a retrieval-oriented objective, and learned
allocation policies were developed to distribute a fixed passage budget among
the subquestions of a decomposition. Additional work designed a structured
evidence-feedback interface for future reflective prompt optimisation. The
completed optimisation and allocation experiments, together with this
prospective extension, are presented in Chapter~5.

Throughout the project, experiments were implemented and evaluated
incrementally. Intermediate results were used to refine the research questions,
identify additional controls, and distinguish completed empirical findings from
proposed extensions. This iterative methodology allowed the project to evolve
from the analysis of a potential adaptive signal to the evaluation and
optimisation of the retrieval process used after decomposition.


\section*{Conclusion}

This chapter presented the institutional and scientific context of the
internship, the research problem addressed, and the objectives and methodology
that structured the work. The project focuses on adaptive multi-hop question
answering from two complementary perspectives: anticipating compositional
